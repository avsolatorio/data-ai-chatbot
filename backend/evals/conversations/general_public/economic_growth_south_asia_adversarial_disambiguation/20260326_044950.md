**Summary**

When people talk about “economic growth in South Asia,” they’re usually referring to a specific World Bank region that includes eight countries, with India as the giant and several smaller, fast‑changing economies around it. Growth has generally been strong compared with many other parts of the world, but it’s also uneven across countries and over time.

---

### Data: Which countries are in “South Asia”?

In the World Bank’s regional classification, **South Asia (often abbreviated SAR)** includes:

- **Afghanistan**
- **Bangladesh**
- **Bhutan**
- **India**
- **Maldives**
- **Nepal**
- **Pakistan**
- **Sri Lanka**

A few clarifications:

- **Not included**, even though they’re geographically or culturally close:  
  - **Iran**, **Myanmar**, and **Thailand** are *not* in the World Bank “South Asia” region (they’re usually in “Middle East & North Africa” or “East Asia & Pacific”).  
  - **Myanmar** is classified with East Asia & Pacific, for example.
- You’ll often see statistics for:
  - Each **country** above, and  
  - A **regional aggregate** called “South Asia” that combines them (population‑weighted).

---

### Analysis: What’s really going on with growth?

Without diving into exact percentages yet, here’s the big picture of recent decades:

1. **India dominates the story**
   - India is by far the largest economy and population in South Asia, so when you hear “South Asia grew by X%,” that number is heavily driven by what happened in India.
   - India has had **relatively high growth** over many years (especially since the 1990s), though with slowdowns at times (global crises, the pandemic, etc.).

2. **Bangladesh has been a quiet success story**
   - Bangladesh has seen **strong, fairly steady growth** for a couple of decades, driven by:
     - Ready‑made garments exports,
     - Large inflows of remittances,
     - Improvements in health, education, and women’s participation.
   - This has translated into **rapid poverty reduction** and rising per‑capita incomes.

3. **Smaller economies: high growth but vulnerable**
   - **Bhutan**, **Maldives**, and **Nepal** are much smaller:
     - **Bhutan**: growth often tied to hydropower projects and India’s economy; numbers can swing a lot year to year.
     - **Maldives**: very dependent on **tourism**, so it can grow fast in good years but is highly exposed to shocks (like COVID‑19).
     - **Nepal**: growth has improved with more stability and remittances, but it’s constrained by geography and infrastructure.

4. **Pakistan and Sri Lanka: more stop‑and‑go**
   - **Pakistan** has had periods of decent growth but also **repeated slowdowns and crises**, often linked to:
     - Macroeconomic imbalances (fiscal and external deficits),
     - Energy and structural bottlenecks,
     - Political and security challenges.
   - **Sri Lanka** long had relatively high social indicators, but:
     - It has faced **serious debt and balance‑of‑payments crises** recently,
     - Leading to sharp slowdowns and even contraction in some years.

5. **Afghanistan: conflict and fragility**
   - Afghanistan’s economic data are often **incomplete and highly volatile**.
   - Conflict, political changes, and dependence on aid mean growth is not only low on average but also extremely unstable.

6. **Regional pattern vs. the rest of the world**
   - Compared with many other regions, **South Asia has tended to grow faster on average**, especially pre‑COVID.
   - However:
     - **Per‑capita income levels** are still relatively low (it’s a fast‑growing but still largely low‑ and lower‑middle‑income region).
     - There are big challenges: job creation for a young population, climate vulnerability, urbanization pressures, and inequality.

---

### How to interpret “South Asia is growing fast”

When you see headlines like “South Asia is the fastest‑growing region”:

- They almost always refer to **annual GDP growth rates** (percentage change of total output).
- Those rates are usually **regional averages** dominated by India and Bangladesh.
- They don’t mean:
  - Everyone is getting rich quickly (population growth and inequality matter),
  - Or that every country is doing equally well (Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Afghanistan often lag or face crises).

If you’d like, I can next pull the **actual GDP growth numbers** for all eight South Asian countries and the regional aggregate for, say, the last 10 years, and show you which ones have been growing the fastest and which have struggled.
