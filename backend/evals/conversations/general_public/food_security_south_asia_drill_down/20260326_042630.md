### 2. Nutrition quality, not just calories

- **Low in diverse, nutrient‑rich foods** (fruits, vegetables, pulses, animal‑source foods)  
- **High in cheap, refined staples and fats**

Consequences seen across South Asia include:

- **Child undernutrition** (stunting and wasting) still above global averages in several countries.  
- **Micronutrient deficiencies** (iron, vitamin A, etc.) common among women and children.  
- **Rising overweight and diabetes** in cities, especially among adults, due to sedentary lifestyles and processed foods.

So the challenge is not only “enough food” but **“enough healthy food”**.

### 3. Who is most at risk?

Within South Asian countries, the highest risks tend to be among:

- **Rural poor households**, particularly landless laborers and smallholders hit by crop failures.  
- **Urban informal workers**, whose incomes are very sensitive to price spikes and lockdowns.  
- **Women and children**, who often eat last or less within households and have higher nutrient needs.  
- **People in fragile and conflict‑affected areas**, where markets and services are disrupted.

Afghanistan is an extreme example: with about <claim id="74b3a6a6" policy="auto">11.7</claim> million people undernourished and a prevalence near <claim id="920c4d06" policy="auto">28.1</claim>% (2022–2024 average), **chronic food insecurity is widespread**.

### 4. What is driving the problem?

Key drivers in South Asia include:

- **Poverty and inequality** – Low and unstable incomes limit what households can buy, especially when prices rise.  
- **Climate and environmental stress** – Floods in Pakistan and Bangladesh, droughts and heat waves in India and Afghanistan, glacial melt in mountain areas.  
- **Food price volatility** – Global and local price spikes (e.g., in wheat, rice, cooking oil) hit poor households hardest.  
- **Agricultural constraints** – Small landholdings, low productivity in rain‑fed areas, and limited access to irrigation, credit, and markets.  
- **Gender and social norms** – Women often have less access to land, income, and decision‑making, which affects child feeding and health.
