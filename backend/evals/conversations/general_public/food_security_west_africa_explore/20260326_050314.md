    - Therapeutic milks (F‑75, F‑100),
    - Careful medical management (fluids, antibiotics, treatment of underlying disease),
    - Transition to RUTF and then outpatient follow‑up.

**In Senegal and Côte d’Ivoire:**  
- CMAM has been progressively integrated into **primary health care** and **district health systems**, often with support from UNICEF, WFP, WHO, and NGOs.  
- There are **national protocols** for acute malnutrition management (aligned with WHO guidelines), and training for health workers and community volunteers.

## 2. Prevention through maternal, infant, and young child nutrition (MIYCN)

Because wasting is closely linked to poor diets and illness in early life, both countries emphasize prevention in the first 1,000 days (pregnancy to age 2).

**Typical components:**

- **Promotion of exclusive breastfeeding (0–6 months)**
  - Counseling during antenatal visits, postnatal care, and community sessions.
  - Support for early initiation of breastfeeding (within the first hour after birth).

- **Complementary feeding (6–23 months)**
  - Education on:
    - Minimum meal frequency,
    - Dietary diversity (using local foods),
    - Safe food preparation and feeding practices.
  - Often delivered via:
    - Group sessions in communities,
    - Growth monitoring and promotion days,
    - Mother support groups.

- **Micronutrient interventions**
  - Vitamin A supplementation for children 6–59 months,
  - Iron and folic acid for pregnant women,
  - Sometimes home fortification with small‑quantity lipid‑based nutrient supplements or micronutrient powders in high‑risk areas.

- **Social and behavior change communication (SBCC)**
  - Radio, community drama, home visits, and group talks to change norms around:
    - Child feeding,
    - Hygiene,
    - Health‑seeking behavior.

Both Senegal and Côte d’Ivoire include MIYCN in their **national nutrition policies/strategies**, and these activities are implemented through health facilities and community platforms.

## 3. Health, WASH, and disease control (addressing underlying causes)

Wasting is often triggered or worsened by infections and poor water/sanitation. So, nutrition programs are linked with:

- **Integrated management of childhood illnesses (IMCI)**
  - Early treatment of diarrhea, pneumonia, malaria, etc.
  - Counseling on feeding during and after illness.

- **Vaccination**
  - Routine immunization and campaigns (e.g., measles, polio) reduce infection burden that can lead to wasting.

- **Water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH)**
  - Promotion of:
    - Handwashing with soap,
    - Safe disposal of feces,
    - Use of safe drinking water.
  - In some areas, projects combine nutrition and WASH (e.g., “nutrition‑sensitive WASH” interventions).

In both countries, national nutrition plans usually highlight **multi‑sectoral coordination** (health, water, agriculture, social protection, education) to tackle these underlying factors.

## 4. Food assistance and safety nets in high‑risk areas

Wasting spikes during **lean seasons**, **droughts**, **floods**, **conflict**, or **price shocks**. In response, Senegal and Côte d’Ivoire have, at different times, used:

- **Targeted supplementary feeding**
  - Fortified blended foods or specialized nutritious foods (e.g., fortified flours, ready‑to‑use supplementary foods) for:
    - Moderately wasted children,
    - Pregnant and lactating women in vulnerable households.

- **General food distributions or cash transfers**
  - Implemented in crisis‑affected or chronically food‑insecure zones, often by:
    - WFP,
    - National social safety net programs,
    - NGOs.
  - Transfers can be:
    - In‑kind food,
    - Cash or vouchers (sometimes “nutrition‑sensitive” cash transfers that encourage health/nutrition service use).

- **School feeding programs**
  - Not directly a wasting treatment, but:
    - Provide a daily meal to school‑age children,
    - Support household food security,
    - Sometimes include nutrition education.
