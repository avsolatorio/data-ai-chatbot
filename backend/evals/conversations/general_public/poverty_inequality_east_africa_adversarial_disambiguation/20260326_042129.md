So Kenya, Tanzania, and Uganda are in the **moderate‑to‑high inequality** range.

## Analysis (what this means in plain language)

### 1. Poverty is still widespread, especially in rural areas
Even though the region has grown economically, the share of people living on very little money is still high:

- In **Kenya**, with around **one in three** people in extreme poverty, many households:
  - Depend on small farms and informal work  
  - Are vulnerable to droughts, food price shocks, and health emergencies  
- In **Tanzania** and **Uganda**, the share in extreme poverty is even higher (around **two in five** or more), which means:
  - Basic needs (enough food, safe water, decent housing) are often not secure  
  - Many children grow up with limited access to quality education and health care  

In practice, this looks like:
- Families skipping meals or relying heavily on one staple food  
- Children leaving school early to help at home or work  
- People unable to pay for medicine or transport to clinics  

### 2. Inequality: growth is not shared equally
The Gini numbers show that **income is unevenly distributed**:

- In cities like **Nairobi**, **Dar es Salaam**, and **Kampala**, you see:
  - A growing middle class and wealthy elites  
  - At the same time, large informal settlements with poor housing and services  
- In rural areas, many people:
  - Rely on rain‑fed agriculture  
  - Have limited access to markets, roads, electricity, and finance  

This means that **even when the economy grows**, the benefits often go more to:
- People with land in good locations  
- Those with higher education  
- Those connected to better‑paying jobs or businesses  

Others see **slow or no improvement**, which keeps inequality relatively high.

### 3. Different countries, somewhat similar challenges
- **Kenya**
  - Extreme poverty is high but somewhat **lower than in Tanzania and Uganda**.  
  - Inequality is **significant but not extreme** (Gini in the high‑30s to around 40).  
  - There is a strong urban–rural divide and regional differences (e.g., arid and semi‑arid lands often poorer).

- **Tanzania**
  - A **larger share of the population is extremely poor** than in Kenya.  
  - Inequality is similar or slightly higher than Kenya’s.  
  - Many people depend on subsistence farming; infrastructure gaps are large in rural areas.

- **Uganda**
  - Extreme poverty is also **very high**, and inequality is **on the high side** in the region.  
  - Rapid population growth makes it harder for incomes per person to rise quickly.

### 4. Why is it like this? (key drivers in simple terms)

Common factors across the region:

- **Agriculture dependence and climate shocks**  
  Many poor households rely on small farms. Droughts, floods, and changing rainfall patterns can wipe out harvests, pushing people deeper into poverty.

- **Fast population growth**  
  The number of people is growing quickly. Even if the economy grows, it has to “stretch” across more people, which can slow improvements per person.

- **Limited good jobs**  
  Many people work in informal jobs (street vending, casual labor, very small businesses) with:
  - Low and unstable pay  
  - No social protection (no unemployment insurance, pensions, etc.)

- **Unequal access to education and services**  
  Where you are born matters a lot:
  - Children in remote rural areas or poorer neighborhoods often get lower‑quality schooling.  
  - Health services can be far away or expensive.  
  - This makes it hard to “move up” even if you work hard.

- **Infrastructure gaps**  
  Poor roads, limited electricity, and weak internet in rural areas mean:
  - Farmers can’t easily get products to market  
  - Businesses are more expensive to run  
  - Investment tends to concentrate in cities

### 5. The good news: there has been progress, but it is uneven
Over the last 20–30 years, these countries have:
- Increased school enrollment  
- Expanded basic health services  
- Grown their economies overall  

This has **reduced poverty compared with the 1990s**, but:
- Many people are still just above the poverty line and can fall back with a shock.  
- Inequality remains high, which can slow poverty reduction and create social tensions.
