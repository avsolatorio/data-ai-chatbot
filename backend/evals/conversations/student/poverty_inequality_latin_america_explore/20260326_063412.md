## 1. Core poverty concepts

### 1.1 Monetary (income/consumption) poverty

These are the concepts most often used in World Bank and regional statistics:

- **Poverty headcount ratio (incidence of poverty)**  
  - Share of people whose income or consumption is below a predefined **poverty line**.  
  - Examples of commonly used lines:
    - **International extreme poverty line** (currently $2.15/day in 2017 PPP; newer work uses updated PPPs and additional lines like $3.00, $4.20, $8.30 in 2021 PPP).  
    - **National poverty line** (defined by each country based on local prices and norms).  
  - Answers: *“What proportion of the population is poor?”*

- **Poverty gap**  
  - Measures how far, on average, poor people are below the poverty line (as a percentage of that line).  
  - Captures **depth** of poverty, not just how many are poor.  
  - Two countries can have the same headcount but very different gaps (one with many people just below the line, another with people far below it).

- **Squared poverty gap / severity of poverty**  
  - Gives more weight to those furthest below the line.  
  - Useful when you care especially about the **very poorest of the poor**.

- **Absolute vs. relative poverty**
  - **Absolute poverty**: uses a fixed standard in real terms (e.g., $2.15/day PPP). Most global comparatives use this.
  - **Relative poverty**: defined relative to the distribution in a country (e.g., below 50% of median income). Common in OECD discussions, sometimes in Latin American debates on inequality and social inclusion.

### 1.2 Multidimensional poverty

- Recognizes that poverty is not only about money, but also about **access to services and capabilities**.
- Typical dimensions:
  - Education (years of schooling, school attendance)
  - Health (nutrition, child mortality, access to health services)
  - Living standards (electricity, water, sanitation, housing quality, assets, internet)
  - Sometimes work, social protection, or security
- **Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)** and related measures:
  - Count people who are deprived in multiple dimensions simultaneously.
  - Key outputs:
    - Headcount of multidimensionally poor
    - Intensity (average number of deprivations among the poor)
- In Latin America, ECLAC and many national statistical offices also use **multidimensional poverty indices** tailored to local priorities.

<hr>

## 2. Inequality concepts

### 2.1 Income/consumption inequality

- **Gini coefficient**
  - Ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality) — often expressed as 0–100.
  - Summarizes the overall dispersion of income/consumption.
  - Very common in Latin America; the region is often described as one of the most unequal in the world by this metric.

- **Income shares / Palma ratio**
  - **Income (or consumption) share by decile/quintile**:
    - Share of total income going to the poorest 20%, richest 10%, etc.
  - **Palma ratio**:
    - Ratio of the income share of the richest 10% to that of the poorest 40%.
  - Useful because they are more interpretable and highlight **top vs. bottom**.

- **Percentile ratios (P90/P10, P50/P10, etc.)**
  - Compare incomes at different points in the distribution.
  - Example: P90/P10 ratio shows how many times richer a person near the top is than a person near the bottom.

### 2.2 Wealth inequality

- Wealth (assets minus debts) is usually **more unequal** than income.
- Data are weaker, but concepts are similar: Gini for wealth, wealth shares of top 1%, 10%, etc.
- In Latin America, wealth inequality is often linked to:
  - Land ownership
  - Financial assets
  - Housing and inheritance

### 2.3 Inequality of opportunity

- Focuses on differences in outcomes that are due to **circumstances beyond an individual’s control**:
  - Family background
  - Gender
  - Race/ethnicity
  - Place of birth / region
  - Parents’ education and income
- Measures:
  - Decomposition of inequality into “circumstance” vs. “effort” components.
  - Gaps in outcomes (e.g., schooling, earnings) across groups.
- Highly relevant in Latin America where **ethnic, racial, territorial, and gender inequalities** are strong.

<hr>

## 3. Regional context: Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC)

Conceptually important features of the region:

- **Historically high inequality**  
  - LAC is often cited as one of the most unequal regions (high Gini, large income shares at the top).
  - Strong legacies of **colonialism, racial stratification, and unequal land distribution**.

- **Middle-income region with persistent poverty pockets**
  - Many countries are upper-middle income, yet:
    - Significant **extreme poverty** remains in some countries and subregions.
    - Large shares of the population hover just above poverty lines, vulnerable to shocks.

- **Vulnerability and the “near-poor” / middle class**
  - The expansion of a “middle class” in the 2000s was often fragile.
  - Concepts like **vulnerable non-poor** (just above the poverty line) are key to understanding setback risks (e.g., during COVID-19).

- **Structural inequalities**
  - Rural vs. urban gaps, especially in access to services and quality jobs.
  - Indigenous and Afro-descendant populations facing higher poverty and exclusion.
  - Gender gaps in labor force participation, wages, unpaid care work.

## 6. Conceptual links you may want to highlight

- **Poverty vs. inequality**  
  - A country can reduce poverty while inequality remains high, if growth benefits poor and rich but the rich gain more.
  - Conversely, reducing inequality (e.g., through transfers or better jobs for the poor) can accelerate poverty reduction.

- **Growth, redistribution, and poverty**
  - Poverty reduction comes from:
    - **Growth** in average income/consumption, and
    - **Changes in distribution** (who benefits from growth).
  - Decompositions (growth vs. redistribution components) are often used in Latin America.

- **Shocks and resilience**
  - Macroeconomic crises, pandemics, climate events can push vulnerable groups back into poverty.
  - Concepts of **resilience, social protection systems, and adaptive capacity** are increasingly central.

- **Intergenerational aspects**
  - How parental education, income, and location affect children’s outcomes.
  - Related to **social mobility** and inequality of opportunity.

### 2.2 Wealth inequality

- Wealth (assets minus debts) is usually **more unequal** than income.
- Data are weaker, but concepts are similar: Gini for wealth, wealth shares of top 1%, 10%, etc.
- In Latin America, wealth inequality is often linked to:
  - Land ownership
  - Financial assets
  - Housing and inheritance

### 2.3 Inequality of opportunity

- Focuses on differences in outcomes that are due to **circumstances beyond an individual’s control**:
  - Family background
  - Gender
  - Race/ethnicity
  - Place of birth / region
  - Parents’ education and income
- Measures:
  - Decomposition of inequality into “circumstance” vs. “effort” components.
  - Gaps in outcomes (e.g., schooling, earnings) across groups.
- Highly relevant in Latin America where **ethnic, racial, territorial, and gender inequalities** are strong.

- **Historically high inequality**  
  - LAC is often cited as one of the most unequal regions (high Gini, large income shares at the top).

- **Middle-income region with persistent poverty pockets**
  - Many countries are upper-middle income, yet:
    - Significant **extreme poverty** remains in some countries and subregions.
    - Large shares of the population hover just above poverty lines, vulnerable to shocks.

  - Concepts like **vulnerable non-poor** (just above the poverty line) are key to understanding setback risks (e.g., during COVID-19).
