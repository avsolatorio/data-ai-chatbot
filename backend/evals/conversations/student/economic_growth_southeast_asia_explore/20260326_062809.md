**🧠 Thinking** (stage: interpreting)

Here are the core ideas you’ll want to grasp to understand economic growth and development in the Philippines (or any country) for your studies.

<hr>

### 1. Economic growth vs. economic development

**Economic growth**  
- Narrow, quantitative: an increase in the value of goods and services produced in the economy.  
- Main measure: **GDP** (Gross Domestic Product).  
  - **GDP growth rate (annual %)** tells you how fast the economy is expanding or contracting year to year.  
- Focuses on **“how big/how fast”** the economy is.

**Economic development**  
- Broader, qualitative: improvements in people’s **well‑being and capabilities**, not just income.  
- Includes:
  - Health (life expectancy, child mortality, nutrition)
  - Education (schooling, learning outcomes, skills)
  - Living standards (poverty, jobs, housing, access to water/electricity)
  - Equity (gender gaps, regional disparities, inequality)
  - Voice and institutions (governance, rights, security)

For the Philippines, you’d look at *both*:
- Growth: how GDP and incomes have changed.
- Development: how people’s lives and opportunities have improved.

<hr>

### 2. Key growth concepts (applied to the Philippines)

1. **GDP and GDP per capita**
   - **GDP**: total value of goods and services produced in a year.
   - **GDP per capita**: GDP divided by population; rough measure of average income.
   - Real (constant‑price) GDP and GDP per capita are used to remove inflation and track **real growth** over time.

2. **Growth structure**
   - Which sectors drive growth:
     - **Agriculture** (e.g., rice, coconut, fisheries)
     - **Industry** (manufacturing, construction)
     - **Services** (BPO/IT‑BPM, tourism, finance, trade)
   - For the Philippines, services and remittances from overseas workers are especially important.

3. **Productivity**
   - How much output is produced per worker or per hour worked.
   - Long‑run growth in living standards depends heavily on **productivity growth** (technology, skills, better infrastructure, better management).

4. **Business cycles and shocks**
   - Short‑term ups and downs: recessions, recoveries.
   - In the Philippines, you’d study:
     - Global shocks (e.g., financial crises, commodity price changes)
     - Domestic shocks (typhoons, political changes, pandemics like COVID‑19)

<hr>

### 3. Key development concepts and indicators

1. **Human development**
   - Often summarized by the **Human Development Index (HDI)**:
     - Income (GNI per capita)
     - Health (life expectancy)
     - Education (years of schooling)
   - Philippines is typically classified as **“medium to high human development”**, reflecting progress but with gaps.

2. **Human capital**
   - The stock of health, skills, knowledge, and experience that people have.
   - World Bank measures this with the **Human Capital Index (HCI)**:
     - Survival (child mortality)
     - Schooling (quantity and quality of education)
     - Health (stunting, adult survival)
   - For the Philippines, HCI is used to ask: *“How productive will a child born today be as a future worker, relative to full health and full education?”*

3. **Poverty**
   - **Monetary poverty**:
     - International lines (e.g., $2.15/day, $3.65/day in 2017 PPP)
     - National poverty lines (Philippines Statistics Authority line)
   - **Multidimensional poverty**:
     - Considers deprivations in health, education, and living standards (e.g., overcrowding, lack of sanitation, no electricity).

4. **Inequality**
   - **Income or consumption inequality**:
     - Measured by **Gini coefficient** (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
   - **Inequality of opportunity**:
     - Differences in outcomes due to circumstances beyond people’s control (e.g., gender, birthplace, parents’ education).
   - In the Philippines, you’d look at:
     - Urban vs rural
     - Metro Manila vs other regions
     - Gender gaps in employment and wages

5. **Employment and labor markets**
   - Unemployment rate, underemployment, labor force participation.
   - Quality of jobs: formal vs informal, wage vs self‑employment, job security, social protection.
   - Remittances and overseas work are central to the Philippine labor story.

6. **Structural transformation**
   - Long‑run shift of employment and output from:
     - Agriculture → Industry → Services.
   - Development theory expects:
     - Productivity rising as workers move into more productive sectors.
   - For the Philippines, a key question is whether the shift into services (especially BPO) is broad‑based enough to absorb workers from agriculture and informal sectors.

<hr>

### 4. How these pieces connect in the Philippine context

When you analyze the Philippines, you’re usually asking:

1. **Is growth fast and stable enough?**
   - Examine trends in **real GDP growth** and **GDP per capita growth** over decades.
   - Look at volatility (how often and how deep downturns are).

2. **Is growth inclusive?**
   - Do poverty rates fall when GDP grows?
   - Does inequality (Gini) improve or worsen?
   - Who benefits: urban vs rural, rich vs poor regions, men vs women?

3. **Is human capital keeping up?**
   - Are education outcomes (learning, not just enrollment) improving?
   - Are health indicators (stunting, maternal mortality) improving?
   - How does the Philippines score on HCI and HDI compared to its ASEAN peers?

4. **Is the economic structure changing in a way that supports long‑term development?**
   - Are more productive, higher‑value‑added sectors growing?
   - Is there enough investment in infrastructure, innovation, and institutions?

5. **Are institutions and governance supporting development?**
   - Quality of public services, rule of law, corruption control.
   - Effectiveness of social protection systems (e.g., 4Ps conditional cash transfers).
