**🧠 Thinking** (stage: interpreting)

When you see warnings about comparability for these regions, the main issue is that “the same” label may refer to slightly different geographic or conceptual units across datasets. Here’s how to handle that systematically.

**Data:**

You’re working with regions such as:

- Delta (Delta State)
- Niger (Niger State)
- South West (geopolitical zone)
- Urban Lagos (urban areas within Lagos State)

No numeric values are involved here, so there are no claim‑tagged observations to report.

<hr>

**Analysis:**

### 1. Check what each dataset actually means by the term

For every dataset, look for:

- **Geographic definitions / shapefiles**
  - Is “Delta” defined as the **current state boundary**, or an older boundary?
  - Is “South West” exactly the six states (Lagos, Oyo, Ogun, Osun, Ondo, Ekiti), or are there any exceptions?
  - For “urban Lagos”, is the definition based on:
    - The national statistical office’s urban/rural classification?
    - Population density thresholds?
    - Administrative units (e.g., certain LGAs only)?

- **Time of definition**
  - Nigeria has had some boundary changes historically; older surveys may use older state or LGA boundaries.
  - If your datasets are from very different years, check if any **re-districting** or **LGA creation/merger** is mentioned in documentation.

**What to do:**  
Create a small “definitions” table in your report methods section that states, for each dataset, how these regions are defined.

<hr>

### 2. Watch for differences in level of aggregation

- **State vs. zone vs. urban/rural**:
  - “Delta” and “Niger” are **states**.
  - “South West” is a **multi‑state zone**.
  - “Urban Lagos” is **a subset of Lagos State**.

Different datasets may:

- Provide **state‑level** data (Delta, Niger, Lagos, etc.).
- Provide **zone‑level** data only (South West vs other zones).
- Provide **urban/rural by state or by zone**, or only national urban/rural.

**Risks:**

- Comparing “South West” from one dataset to the **average of its six states** from another is usually OK but not exact, because:
  - The zone may be weighted by population in a specific way.
  - Some datasets may exclude or reclassify border areas.

**What to do:**

- Where possible, **reconstruct comparable units**:
  - If Dataset A gives state‑level data, and Dataset B gives zone‑level data, aggregate A’s states (population‑weighted) to approximate the zone.
- If you can’t reconstruct, **label clearly**:
  - “South West (DHS zone definition)” vs. “South West (population‑weighted average of six states, using NBS population estimates)”.

<hr>

### 3. Handle “urban Lagos” carefully

This is often the most problematic:

- **Urban definition**:
  - DHS, MICS, and national surveys may all use the term “urban”, but their criteria can differ slightly.
  - One survey might reclassify some peri‑urban LGAs as rural; another might keep them as urban.

- **Administrative coverage**:
  - Some surveys oversample “metropolitan” LGAs (e.g., Lagos Mainland, Lagos Island) and under‑sample outer LGAs.
  - Another dataset (e.g., census‑based) might apply a stricter boundary for what counts as “Lagos urban”.

**What to do:**

- In your methods, add a short statement like:
  - “Urban Lagos is defined according to the urban/rural classification used in the 2018 DHS; this may not fully align with urban classifications used in other datasets.”
- Avoid interpreting small differences (e.g., 1–2 percentage points) between surveys as meaningful changes for “urban Lagos” unless you are sure the definitions match.

<hr>

### 4. Align time frames and administrative changes

- **Year alignment**:
  - If you compare 2010 data for “Delta State” from one source with 2022 data from another, note that:
    - Population distribution, urbanization, and even boundaries may have changed.
- **Boundary changes / LGA splits**:
  - If a dataset uses an older LGA structure, zone or state aggregates might not match newer ones exactly.

**What to do:**

- When using **trend analysis**, keep comparisons **within the same survey series** where possible (e.g., DHS 2013 vs DHS 2018).
- When mixing sources, include a caveat:
  - “State boundaries and urban/rural classifications may differ slightly between sources; comparisons should be interpreted as indicative rather than exact.”

<hr>

### 5. Practical wording for comparability warnings

You can use short, clear notes in your report, for example:

- For Delta and Niger:
  - “Values for Delta and Niger refer to the respective Nigerian states as defined in each survey year; minor differences in state boundaries or sampling frames across sources may affect precise comparability.”

- For South West:
  - “The South West region is defined as the six‑state geopolitical zone (Lagos, Oyo, Ogun, Osun, Ondo, Ekiti). In datasets where only zone‑level estimates are available, we use the survey’s own South West definition; in others, we approximate the zone using a population‑weighted average of the six states.”

- For urban Lagos:
  - “Urban Lagos refers to areas classified as ‘urban’ within Lagos State in each dataset. Because the criteria for ‘urban’ can differ slightly across sources, comparisons of urban Lagos between datasets should be interpreted with caution.”

<hr>

### 6. When to avoid direct comparison

You should **avoid direct numeric comparison** (or at least de‑emphasize it) when:

- One dataset uses **state‑level Lagos** and another uses **only urban Lagos**, but you don’t have a way to separate urban from rural in the first dataset.
- One dataset uses **South West zone** and another gives only **Lagos State** data, but no data for the other South West states.
- One dataset clearly states it uses a **different administrative classification** (e.g., “metropolitan Lagos area” vs “Lagos State”).

In those cases, describe patterns qualitatively (e.g., “higher than the national average”, “substantially lower than other South West states”) rather than presenting them as precise, directly comparable numbers.
