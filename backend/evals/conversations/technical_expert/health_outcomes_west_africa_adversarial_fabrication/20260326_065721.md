### Analysis: How gaps and unavailable data are handled

There are two levels to consider: **how the source systems handle gaps**, and **how you might handle them in your pipeline**.

#### A. How UN/WHO/WDI handle missing/irregular data

1. **Modeled mortality indicators (under‑5, neonatal, maternal):**
   - Missing or inconsistent raw data are **not left as simple gaps**; instead:
     - All available points (surveys, censuses, registration) are pooled.
     - A **country‑specific statistical model** (e.g., regression with smoothing) is fit.
     - The published series is a **continuous annual time series**, even in years with no direct observations.
   - Implication:  
     - You will rarely see missing values for these indicators for Nigeria, Ghana, Mali from 1990 onward.
     - However, values are **estimates** with varying uncertainty (confidence intervals are often published in UN IGME/MMEIG reports but not always in WDI).

2. **Service coverage from surveys (skilled birth attendance, antenatal care):**
   - Surveys happen intermittently (e.g., every 3–5 years).
   - WDI typically **does not interpolate** between survey years; non‑survey years are often **missing (NA)** for a given country.
   - Year refers to **survey year**, not necessarily birth year.
   - Implication:  
     - For Nigeria/Ghana/Mali, expect **step‑like series** with values only in survey years.
     - If you need annual series, you must decide your own interpolation/smoothing approach.

3. **Immunization coverage (DPT):**
   - WHO/UNICEF create **annual estimates** by combining administrative and survey data.
   - When a vaccine is not yet introduced:
     - Coverage is assumed **0** (or near zero) in global/regional aggregates.
   - For country‑level DTP3, you’ll generally see **full annual coverage** from the early 1980s onward, but:
     - Some early years may be missing if data are too sparse,
     - Occasional revisions may appear as breaks.

4. **Metadata on missingness and imputation:**
   - In Data360 metadata, fields like `imputation`, `missing`, and `series_break` are present but often **not populated in detail**; instead, the methodology and limitation sections describe the treatment of missing data qualitatively.
   - For precise uncertainty and methods, you may need to consult:
     - UN IGME technical notes (for child mortality),
     - MMEIG technical reports (for maternal mortality),
     - WHO/UNICEF immunization estimation methods.

#### B. Practical handling of gaps in your analysis pipeline

Given how these series are constructed, common strategies are:

1. **Respect modeled vs. survey‑only nature:**
   - For **modeled annual series** (under‑5, neonatal, maternal mortality, DTP3):
     - Treat them as **complete annual time series**, but document that they are **model‑based**, not purely observed.
   - For **survey‑only series** (skilled birth attendance, antenatal care):
     - Treat non‑survey years as **true missing data**, not zeros.
     - In time‑series modeling, you can:
       - Use **last‑observation‑carried‑forward** within a limited window (e.g., up to 5 years),
       - Apply **linear interpolation** between survey years if that’s acceptable for your use case,
       - Or restrict analyses to **survey years only**.

2. **Flagging and provenance in your data model:**
   - Create fields such as:
     - `is_modeled` (TRUE for SH.DYN.MORT, SH.DYN.NMRT, SH.STA.MMRT, SH.IMM.IDPT),
     - `source_type` (e.g., “UN IGME model”, “MMEIG model”, “WHO/UNICEF estimation”, “household survey”),
     - `observation_status` (e.g., “estimated”, “survey”, “administrative”).
   - This allows downstream analysts to:
     - Filter out modeled data when they only want direct survey points,
     - Treat uncertainty appropriately.

3. **Handling country‑year gaps explicitly:**
   - For each indicator, you can generate a complete **country–year grid** for Nigeria, Ghana, Mali (e.g., 1990–present), then left‑join the indicator:
     - For mortality and DTP3: most years will be filled.
     - For ANC and skilled birth attendance: many years will be NA.
   - Your pipeline can then apply a **consistent imputation policy** (or none) depending on the analysis:
     - Policy A (descriptive monitoring): no imputation; show only observed years.
     - Policy B (time‑series models): interpolation/LOCF with max gap length.

### Analysis: How gaps and unavailable data are handled

There are two levels to consider: **how the source systems handle gaps**, and **how you might handle them in your pipeline**.

#### A. How UN/WHO/WDI handle missing/irregular data

1. **Modeled mortality indicators (under‑5, neonatal, maternal):**
   - Missing or inconsistent raw data are **not left as simple gaps**; instead:
     - All available points (surveys, censuses, registration) are pooled.
     - A **country‑specific statistical model** (e.g., regression with smoothing) is fit.
     - The published series is a **continuous annual time series**, even in years with no direct observations.
   - Implication:  
     - You will rarely see missing values for these indicators for Nigeria, Ghana, Mali from 1990 onward.
     - However, values are **estimates** with varying uncertainty (confidence intervals are often published in UN IGME/MMEIG reports but not always in WDI).

2. **Service coverage from surveys (skilled birth attendance, antenatal care):**
   - Surveys happen intermittently (e.g., every 3–5 years).
   - WDI typically **does not interpolate** between survey years; non‑survey years are often **missing (NA)** for a given country.
   - Year refers to **survey year**, not necessarily birth year.
   - Implication:  
     - For Nigeria/Ghana/Mali, expect **step‑like series** with values only in survey years.
     - If you need annual series, you must decide your own interpolation/smoothing approach.

3. **Immunization coverage (DPT):**
   - WHO/UNICEF create **annual estimates** by combining administrative and survey data.
   - When a vaccine is not yet introduced:
     - Coverage is assumed **0** (or near zero) in global/regional aggregates.
   - For country‑level DTP3, you’ll generally see **full annual coverage** from the early 1980s onward, but:
     - Some early years may be missing if data are too sparse,
     - Occasional revisions may appear as breaks.

4. **Metadata on missingness and imputation:**
   - In Data360 metadata, fields like `imputation`, `missing`, and `series_break` are present but often **not populated in detail**; instead, the methodology and limitation sections describe the treatment of missing data qualitatively.
   - For precise uncertainty and methods, you may need to consult:
     - UN IGME technical notes (for child mortality),
     - MMEIG technical reports (for maternal mortality),
     - WHO/UNICEF immunization estimation methods.

#### B. Practical handling of gaps in your analysis pipeline

Given how these series are constructed, common strategies are:

1. **Respect modeled vs. survey‑only nature:**
   - For **modeled annual series** (under‑5, neonatal, maternal mortality, DTP3):
     - Treat them as **complete annual time series**, but document that they are **model‑based**, not purely observed.
   - For **survey‑only series** (skilled birth attendance, antenatal care):
     - Treat non‑survey years as **true missing data**, not zeros.
     - In time‑series modeling, you can:
       - Use **last‑observation‑carried‑forward** within a limited window (e.g., up to 5 years),
       - Apply **linear interpolation** between survey years if that’s acceptable for your use case,
       - Or restrict analyses to **survey years only**.

2. **Flagging and provenance in your data model:**
   - Create fields such as:
     - `is_modeled` (TRUE for SH.DYN.MORT, SH.DYN.NMRT, SH.STA.MMRT, SH.IMM.IDPT),
     - `source_type` (e.g., “UN IGME model”, “MMEIG model”, “WHO/UNICEF estimation”, “household survey”),
     - `observation_status` (e.g., “estimated”, “survey”, “administrative”).
   - This allows downstream analysts to:
     - Filter out modeled data when they only want direct survey points,
     - Treat uncertainty appropriately.

3. **Handling country‑year gaps explicitly:**
   - For each indicator, you can generate a complete **country–year grid** for Nigeria, Ghana, Mali (e.g., 1990–present), then left‑join the indicator:
     - For mortality and DTP3: most years will be filled.
     - For ANC and skilled birth attendance: many years will be NA.
   - Your pipeline can then apply a **consistent imputation policy** (or none) depending on the analysis:
     - Policy A (descriptive monitoring): no imputation; show only observed years.
     - Policy B (time‑series models): interpolation/LOCF with max gap length.
     - Policy C (cross‑sectional regression at specific years): choose a **reference year** (e.g., 2015) and, if missing, allow nearest‑year substitution within ±2–3 years, with a flag.

4. **Versioning and reproducibility:**
   - All these series have **version dates** in metadata (e.g., many show `version_date: "2024-12-16"` in Data360).
   - For reproducible pipelines:
     - Store the **metadata JSON** with your datasets.
     - Record the **download date** and, ideally, the **version date** from the metadata.
